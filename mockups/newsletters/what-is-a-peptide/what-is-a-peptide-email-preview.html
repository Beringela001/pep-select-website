Peptides, explained

What is a peptide?

A peptide is a short chain of amino acids. That simple structure gives researchers thousands of different sequences to study.

Start with the building blocks

Amino acids connect in a specific order. The number of links and their sequence determine whether you have a short peptide or a much larger folded protein.

Peptide is a structure, not a drug category

The word peptide describes a molecule made from a short amino-acid chain. It does not automatically mean medicine. Some peptides occur naturally, some are laboratory research compounds, and some peptide-based molecules are developed as regulated drugs. Pep Select products are research compounds, not drugs, supplements, or medical devices.
Why sequence matters

Same building blocks. Different instructions.

Change the amino-acid order and you change the peptide’s shape. Shape influences what the molecule can bind to and how researchers study its behavior.

Two clear examples

These Pep Select compounds show how a short peptide sequence can create a specific research question.
3 amino acids + copper

GHK-Cu

GHK-Cu is a copper-binding tripeptide: three amino acids that form a complex with a copper ion. Researchers study how that complex participates in signaling connected with structural-protein synthesis and matrix remodeling.

Explore GHK-Cu
 
Peptide + cell scaffolding

TB-500

TB-500 is derived from thymosin beta-4 and studied for its interaction with actin. Actin helps cells maintain structure and organize movement, so researchers examine how TB-500-related sequences influence actin assembly and cell motility.

The short version

Amino acids are the building blocks. Peptides are short sequences. Proteins are longer, folded chains. The sequence gives each molecule its research identity.
